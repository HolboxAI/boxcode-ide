Companion to `docs/PLAN.md` (phases) and the "Parity Without Bloat" feature-map design. This file exists so Tier 1/2 work has somewhere concrete to live instead of staying only in a chat conversation.

**Marker convention:** `[ ]` open, `[x]` done (struck through, with what actually happened recorded rather than deleted). `[~]` was added 2026-09-05 for *partially* shipped — introduced because two rows were about to be marked done on the strength of a PR title when the merged diff showed the load-bearing half was still missing.

**Precedent worth knowing before treating the full fork as a blocker:** Cursor's actual first version (v0.1, March 2023) was built on CodeMirror, not a VS Code fork — they only moved to a VSCodium-based fork later, once they had product-market fit. Kiro started as a 3-person internal side project that grew through dogfooding before AWS invested infra behind it. Neither started by solving the heavy fork-build-pipeline problem first. Lean here, on purpose.

## New 2026-09-05: visual-layer follow-ups from the PR #24–#37 review

The 2026-09-05 batch shipped two themes, a global corner-radius bump and Geist as the workbench typeface. Full critique with the arithmetic and the measured counts is in `docs/PLAN.md`, "Design/UX review of the 2026-09-05 batch". These are the concrete follow-ups it produced, ordered by leverage. The honest headline: **the instincts are right, "premium" hasn't landed yet, and the remaining work is small and specific — finish it before adding more visual features.**

- [ ] **Fix the primary-button contrast regression — highest priority in this section, and it's arithmetic, not taste.** `boxcode-dark.json`/`boxcode-light.json` set `button.background`, `activityBarBadge.background` and `menu.selectionBackground` to `#D97706` without touching the paired foreground, which stays `#FFFFFF` in both stock themes (`extensions/theme-defaults/themes/dark_modern.json:12,17`; `light_modern.json:12,17,67`). That is **3.19:1** — below WCAG AA's 4.5:1 for normal text. Both upstream defaults passed (`#0078D4` = 4.53:1, `#005FB8` = 6.31:1), so this is a regression we introduced, in both themes, on the most-clicked control in the product. It is also backwards: the hover state `#B45309` is 5.02:1, so the button gets *more* legible on hover. Two fixes, either sufficient: pair the amber with `#000000` (6.59:1, keeps the exact brand hue) or drop the resting background to `#B45309` with `#92400E` hover. The authors already applied exactly this reasoning to `textLink.foreground` in the light theme (`#92400E`, 7.09:1, with a comment explaining it) — the buttons just didn't get the same pass.
- [ ] **Extend the warm-surface treatment past three keys.** PR #34's surface-elevation instinct is the most sophisticated thing in the batch (`editorWidget.background: #211F1C`, `input.background: #302C27`, `dropdown.background: #332F29` — warm-biased rather than flat gray, which is genuinely what reads as elevation). But it touches 3 keys out of ~230 in `COLOR_THEME_DARK_INITIAL_COLORS`. Editor background, side bar, activity bar, panel, tab bar, list hover and every border are still stock Dark Modern cold gray — so the current result is warm widgets and an amber accent floating on a cold ground, i.e. stock VS Code with a brand color. Apply the same warm-bias rule to the ~15 large-surface backgrounds and borders in both themes, checked against the contrast item above. Contained work, highest visual leverage per hour available right now.
- [ ] **Resolve the half-migrated corner radius — pick an end-state, don't stay in the middle.** `patches/91-ui-rounded-corners.patch` claims retuning the tokens "reaches all of them at once." Measured against the pinned tree it reaches about a third: **254** `border-radius` declarations use `var(--vscode-cornerRadius-*)` vs **525** hardcoded, and the two largest remaining hardcoded buckets are **128 literal `4px`** and **41 literal `6px`** — precisely the values the tokens moved away from (`small` 4→5, `medium` 6→8). In the chat UI specifically, which the patch names as its justification, only **33 of 212** declarations under `src/vs/workbench/contrib/chat/` use the token (~16%). PR #35 migrated 3 of the 525. Net effect: surfaces that previously matched their neighbours at 4px/6px now differ by 1px/2px. The risk is incoherence, not breakage. Either (a) finish it — sweep the 128 literal `4px` onto `cornerRadius.small` and the 41 `6px` onto `cornerRadius.medium`, mechanical, makes the token genuinely systemic; or (b) revert `cornerRadius.small` to 4 and keep only `medium`/`large`/`xLarge` bumped. ## New 2026-09-05: retention-driven items (Tier 2 by priority, new in kind)

From the retention research in `docs/PLAN.md`, "Retention research 2026-09-05". These are new *in kind*, not new features in an existing tier: this backlog has been organised around capability parity (what does a competitor have that we don't), and the research says parity is not what determines whether someone stays. **Read the method caveats in PLAN.md before acting on any of this — Reddit and X were unreachable, so the entire corpus is Hacker News plus vendor primary sources, and quotes should be spot-checked at their permalinks.**

**Re-sequencing, justified against the existing rationale rather than overriding it.** This backlog's Tier 2 ordering put predictive completion first on a claim about how *loved* a feature is. That is a measure of acquisition appeal, not retention, and the research found the two come apart sharply: the #1 churn trigger across every vendor studied is cost/limit transitions, and nobody is documented churning over Tab. So the two items below go *above* Tab in Tier 2. Tab itself stays on the list and gets cheaper, not dropped — see its own re-ranking note above.

- [ ] **A truthful, always-visible cost/usage meter — new #1 in Tier 2.** *Evidence: strong.* Every vendor in the study took its largest public backlash on a pricing or rate-limit transition, not on quality: Cursor's June 2025 change forced [a formal apology and refunds](https://cursor.com/blog/june-2025-pricing); [Copilot's move to usage-based billing](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/github-copilot-is-moving-to-usage-based-billing/) drew 767 points / 553 comments and documented multiplier increases up to 900%; Kiro capped usage within a week of launch. The anger is consistently about **the transition and the opacity**, not the absolute number. And the two live bets are visibly opposed — Cursor removed cost detail from its usage page in Aug 2026 while Claude Code shipped prompt-cache-miss diagnostics in `/cost`; the market punished the first.
  - **This is an advantage of the closed tool set, not just a constraint.** Because boxcode owns every tool and the whole token budget end to end, it can show a truthful per-turn cost breakdown that a tool composed of arbitrary third-party MCP servers structurally cannot. That framing is worth stating publicly.
  - Cheapest retention insurance available to us, and currently absent from every phase and tier in both docs. Also worth writing down now, before it is needed: the remediation playbook that demonstrably worked is Cursor's — name the mistake, refund a specific named window, publish it.
- [ ] **Make the verification loop cheap in context — and position it that way.** *Evidence: strong, and this reframes work already in flight rather than adding new scope.* "The agent can't see the page" is the most-repeated frontend complaint (Adam Argyle, ["Why AI Sucks at Front End"](https://nerdy.dev/why-ai-sucks-at-front-end), 2026-04-12). **But the market objection is not that nobody has it** — Playwright MCP, Chrome DevTools MCP and Antigravity Browser Control all exist and commenters say so dismissively. The two genuinely unsolved parts are narrow and well-attested: the loop **burns context faster than it earns** (boomskats: *"chrome devtools mcp really clutters your context"* — [HN 47500807](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47500807)), and **DOM assertions don't catch visual wrongness** (mrothroc: *"it can't tell you whether the page actually looks right... Colors wrong, layout shifted, spacing off, components overlapping"* — [HN 47504657](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47504657)).
  - Concrete work: a purpose-built, token-efficient page representation for `check_in_browser` — not raw DOM dumps, and not a full screenshot on every step. Measure tokens per verification step and treat it as the headline metric.
  - **Positioning consequence, and it is a caution as much as an opportunity:** claim *"verification that doesn't eat your context window,"* never *"our agent can see."* The second is commoditized, and Cursor took [724 points / 309 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46646777) of public beating in Jan 2026 for over-claiming exactly it.
- [ ] **Ship visibility and an exit for the bundled platform, from day one — an execution constraint on the lock-in strategy, not a challenge to it.** *Evidence: strong.* The bundling strategy is settled and this does not reopen it. But a bundled DB/auth/deploy stack asks for far more trust than an editor does, and the research found trust failures behave differently from every other kind: users returned to Cursor after a pricing scandal, and **nobody in the corpus describes returning to a tool after a trust incident**. Lovable is the precise warning — allthetime: *"The supabase backend is completely inaccessible except through the lovable interface. You don't have a supabase account, you can't manage any of it directly"* ([HN 49288290](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49288290)), escalating a week later to *"crazy levels of lock in built in"* ([HN 49402474](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49402474)). **The rage is not earned by locking users in; it is earned by making their own data unviewable.** Minimum bar: read access to the real database, and a working export, before the bundled stack is promoted to anyone outside the team.
- [ ] **Publish a blast-radius/permissions story before the bundled stack is promoted.** *Evidence: strong for the negative case.* Indirect prompt injection shipped to production in Kiro, Antigravity and Codex within twelve months; Antigravity's four highest-engagement threads are all incidents (drive deletion 544 pts, exfiltration 768 pts, a forced update destroying settings 771 pts, account-ban exposure 333 pts). `boxcode`'s plan mode is already a real capability switch rather than a prompt-level guardrail (`src/tools.rs` — *"the tools that write are not on the model's list at all, so there is nothing to mistakenly approve"*), which is a genuinely stronger design than most of the category. **That is currently an undocumented internal detail. It should be a published position.**

**One hypothesis explicitly recorded as unevidenced, so it doesn't get quietly promoted to a finding.** Design-system and framework fidelity — plausibly our sharpest wedge — has **zero supporting evidence either way**: no substantial source was found on AI failing to respect an *internal* design system, and no 2026 evidence at all on Figma→code fidelity (best available quote is 2025-01-22). The Tailwind evidence actively cuts the other way (Tailwind Labs' 75% engineering layoff, [1457 pts](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46527950), explained in-thread as AI getting *good enough* at CSS to kill the paid component-library business — **do not claim "AI is bad at Tailwind"**). And the strongest counter-argument to our whole positioning must be engaged rather than ignored — josephg: *"Frontend development... is 'what you see is what you get'. There aren't a lot of ways a website can be invisibly broken"* ([HN 49557707](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49557707)). If that is right, frontend was the *first* AI target because its errors are cheap and visible, not because it is underserved, and "frontend engineers need special AI tooling" is a weaker wedge than it looks. **The durable version of the pitch is the verification-cost item above, which survives that objection; "AI is bad at frontend" does not.** Closing this gap is a case for primary research — interview ten frontend developers — not more searching.
